# International Benchmarks for a Qatar Hospital's Policy on De-identifying Patient Data and Sharing It (External AI Diagnostic Tool, Insurers, Health Ministry)

Research date: 22 November 2026. Scope: standards and comparable regimes a Qatari hospital would cite as *benchmarks* (not as applicable law). Qatar's own law (e.g., PDPPL Law No. 13 of 2016, MoPH rules) is out of scope here and covered elsewhere.

Source-labelling conventions used below:
- **[primary, fetched]** = primary text retrieved and read this session.

## 1. Joint Commission International (JCI) hospital standards: MOI chapter, related chapters, and AI/CDS expectations

### Takeaway
The current JCI hospital manual is the **8th edition, effective 1 January 2025**. It renumbered everything into a two-level format (e.g., MOI.01.01), so citations to MOI.1–MOI.13 / "MOI.2" etc. are 6th/7th-edition numbering and are **superseded**. For this policy, the citable JCI hooks are MOI.01.01–MOI.01.04 (access control, protection, retention, training), PCC.01.02 (patient privacy/confidentiality/access), HCT.01.01 (electronic transmission), **HCT.01.03 (clinical decision support tools: selection, implementation, oversight, improvement)**, HCT.01.05 (cybersecurity), GLD.05.00 (contract oversight), QPS.03.00–03.02 (data aggregation and validation, including external databases) and GLD.09.00 / HRP (research). JCI has no dedicated AI standard; the closest accreditation-body AI benchmark is the US Joint Commission + CHAI "Responsible Use of AI in Healthcare" (RUAIH) guidance (Sept 2025) and its voluntary RUAIH certification (launched 2026).

### Inferences
- A Qatar policy should cite **JCI 8th ed. MOI.01.01 (role-based access), MOI.01.02 (protection incl. cyber), MOI.01.03 (retention), MOI.01.04 (training), PCC.01.02 (privacy), HCT.01.01 (electronic transmission to external parties), HCT.01.03 (CDS/AI tool lifecycle governance) and GLD.05.00 (vendor contract monitoring)** as the accreditation anchors for sharing de-identified data with an AI vendor.
- HCT.01.03 gives the hospital an accreditation basis to require a formal selection → validation → go-live → monitoring → improvement process for the external AI diagnostic tool; the RUAIH seven elements can be used to flesh out what "oversight" means in practice.
- QPS.03.00 ("participation in external databases") supports aggregated/de-identified reporting to the Ministry as a quality activity.
- Because RUAIH is a US Joint Commission product (not JCI), cite it as a "best-practice benchmark," not an accreditation requirement for a JCI-accredited Qatar hospital.

### Gaps
- The JCI standards list gives standard statements only; the **measurable elements (MEs)** and intent statements for MOI.01.01–01.04, PCC.01.02 and HCT.01.03 (e.g., specific release-of-information, de-identification or data-use-agreement MEs) are in the paid manual and were not retrieved.
- No evidence found that JCI (as opposed to the US Joint Commission) has issued an AI-specific standard, survey guidance or certification as of Nov 2026.
- JCI's 8th-edition Standards Interpretation FAQ exists but was not reviewed. — [JCI SIG FAQ](https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/SIG_FAQ-Hospitals_8th_Edition?v=2c8ce113)

## 2. ISO/IEC standards: what each contributes (27001, 27002, 27701, 27799, 42001, 20889, 25237, plus 27559 and 42005)

### Takeaway
The ISO stack gives a layered, certifiable architecture: **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** (certifiable ISMS) + **27002:2022** (control guidance) + **ISO 27799:2025** (health-sector application of 27002; supersedes 27799:2016) for security; **ISO/IEC 27701:2025** (now a *standalone*, certifiable privacy management system; supersedes the 2019 extension) for privacy; **ISO/IEC 42001:2023** (certifiable AI management system) + **ISO/IEC 42005:2025** (AI impact assessment guidance) for AI governance; and **ISO/IEC 20889:2018** (terminology and classification of de-identification techniques), **ISO/IEC 27559:2022** (de-identification governance framework) and **ISO 25237:2017** (health pseudonymisation; revision at DIS stage in 2026) for de-identification method. Full ISO texts are paywalled; details below are from ISO catalogue pages and secondary summaries unless stated.

### Cited Findings
**ISO 27799 (health informatics security)**
- **ISO 27799:2025**, "Health informatics — Information security controls in health based on ISO/IEC 27002," Edition 3; provides information security controls, including implementation guidance, for health organizations; replaces ISO 27799:2016 (title changed from "information security management in health"). — [ISO catalogue page](https://www.iso.org/standard/84647.html); [iTeh listing](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/iso/ce4938c7-6fa0-484e-8fa1-8913515796ed/iso-27799-2025); superseded edition: [ISO 27799:2016 listing](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/iso/76f5fb7d-ec8a-4c1d-b91e-417790392f72/iso-27799-2016) [secondary/catalogue]

**ISO/IEC 27701 (privacy information management)**
- **ISO/IEC 27701:2025** was published in October 2025 (14 Oct 2025 per several sources) and replaces ISO/IEC 27701:2019; it is now a standalone Privacy Information Management System (PIMS) standard that no longer requires ISO/IEC 27001 as a prerequisite; controller and processor controls are consolidated in Annex A. — [BSI](https://www.bsigroup.com/en-IL/products-and-services/standards-services/iso-iec-27701-key-changes-and-guidance/); [Abilene Academy](https://www.abileneacademy.ch/en/blog/iso-27701-2025-the-complete-guide); [A-LIGN](https://www.a-lign.com/articles/iso-27701-updates-what-you-need-to-know) [secondary]
- A 27701 certificate does not replace legal obligations (e.g., GDPR). — [Abilene Academy](https://www.abileneacademy.ch/en/blog/iso-27701-2025-the-complete-guide) [secondary]

**ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and 27002:2022 [primary catalogue; content from well-established standard structure, not re-fetched]**
- ISO/IEC 27001:2022 is the certifiable ISMS requirements standard; its Annex A lists 93 controls in four themes (organizational, people, physical, technological), with implementation guidance in ISO/IEC 27002:2022. Controls most relevant to this policy: 5.12 classification of information; 5.14 information transfer; 5.15 access control; 5.19–5.23 supplier relationships and cloud services; 5.34 privacy and protection of PII; 8.10 information deletion; **8.11 data masking** (pseudonymisation/anonymisation as a control); 8.12 data leakage prevention; 8.15 logging; 8.24 use of cryptography. — [ISO/IEC 27001 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/27001); [ISO/IEC 27002:2022 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/75652.html) [primary, not re-fetched]

**ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (AI management system) and ISO/IEC 42005:2025 (AI impact assessment)**
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (published Dec 2023) is the certifiable AI management system standard; clauses 6.1.2–6.1.4 require AI risk assessment, AI risk treatment and **AI system impact assessment**; Annex A controls cover AI policy, internal organization, resources, impact assessment, AI system life cycle, **data for AI systems (quality, provenance, preparation)**, information for interested parties, use of AI systems, and **third-party and customer relationships** (relevant to buying an external AI tool). — [ISO/IEC 42001 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html) [primary, not re-fetched]
- **ISO/IEC 42005:2025** (Edition 1, published May 2025) gives guidance for conducting AI system impact assessments across the lifecycle (design to post-market monitoring); not certifiable; designed to complement ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 38507 and ISO/IEC 23894 and to integrate with privacy and human-rights impact assessments rather than duplicate them. — [ISO 42005 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/42005); [CMS law summary](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/iso-iec-42005-2025-a-new-blueprint-for-legal-and-commercial-leaders-navigating-ai-risk-and-governance) [catalogue + secondary]

**De-identification standards**
- **ISO/IEC 20889:2018**, "Privacy enhancing data de-identification terminology and classification of techniques," is the reference vocabulary/classification for de-identification techniques (e.g., suppression, generalization, randomization, pseudonymization, and formal models such as k-anonymity and differential privacy). — [IHE De-Identification Handbook references](https://profiles.ihe.net/ITI/DeId/references.html) [secondary for content listing]
- **ISO 25237:2017**, "Health informatics — Pseudonymization": health-specific principles and requirements for pseudonymisation services, including trusted-third-party models and controlled re-identification; ISO 25237 treats pseudonymization as a subcategory of de-identification. A revision is at draft stage (prEN ISO 25237 / ISO/DIS 25237:2026), not yet published. — [iTeh EN ISO 25237:2017 listing](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/cen/5ecd6d57-6046-4c61-a73f-fcd0d23687fe/en-iso-25237-2017); [ISO 25237:2017 sample PDF](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/63553/a46a0e310d174533bebadb48bfa1093a/ISO-25237-2017.pdf) [catalogue/secondary]
- **ISO/IEC 27559:2022**, "Privacy enhancing data de-identification framework," provides a governance/risk framework (context, data and identifiability assessment, de-identification governance) that complements the 20889 technique catalogue. — [ISO/IEC 27559 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/71677.html) [primary catalogue, not re-fetched]

### Inferences
- Policy architecture benchmark: certify (or align) the hospital ISMS to **27001:2022 with 27799:2025 health controls**, the privacy program to **27701:2025**, and the AI governance program to **42001:2023**, using **42005:2025** as the template for the AI-tool impact assessment and **20889/27559/25237** to specify how data are de-identified before release.
- Requiring the external AI vendor to hold ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 42001 certification (and 27701 if processing identifiable data) is a defensible, internationally recognised contractual baseline.
- Since 27701:2025 decoupled from 27001, a policy should cite the edition explicitly; a 27701:2019 certificate is tied to an older extension model.

### Gaps
- Exact clause/control numbers in ISO 27799:2025, 27701:2025 Annex A and 42001 Annex A (A.x.y) were not verified from the normative text (paywalled); ISO 27799:2025's exact publication month is unclear (ISO page copyright reads 2026).
- Transition deadlines for 27701:2019 → 2025 certificates were not confirmed.
- ISO/IEC 27001:2022 transition deadline (widely reported as 31 Oct 2025) not re-verified this session.

## 3. De-identification methods and thresholds (HIPAA as reference point; k-anonymity; small-cell rules; ICO; WP29 05/2014 and its 2026 successor)

### Takeaway
Two families of benchmarks: **rule-based** (HIPAA Safe Harbor's 18 identifiers; public-body small-cell rules: CMS suppress 1–10, NHS HES suppress 1–7 and round to nearest 5, Canadian practice <5) and **risk-based** (HIPAA Expert Determination "very small" risk; ICO "motivated intruder" and spectrum of identifiability; EU WP29 three tests of singling out/linkability/inference, now being replaced by **EDPB draft Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation** with "no record isolation, no linkage, no inference"). Pseudonymised data remain personal data for the discloser (ICO; EDPB; CJEU *EDPS v SRB*, 2025), though they may be anonymous in the hands of a recipient who cannot reasonably re-identify.

### Cited Findings
**HIPAA Privacy Rule, 45 CFR 164.514 (US; reference point only) [primary, fetched via Cornell LII]**
- 164.514(a): information is not individually identifiable if it does not identify an individual and there is "no reasonable basis to believe that the information can be used to identify an individual." — [45 CFR 164.514 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.514)
- **Expert Determination, 164.514(b)(1)**: a qualified expert determines "the risk is very small that the information could be used, alone or in combination with other reasonably available information, by an anticipated recipient to identify an individual," and documents the methods and results. — [45 CFR 164.514 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.514)
- **Safe Harbor, 164.514(b)(2)(i)**: remove 18 identifiers (A–R) of the individual and of relatives, employers or household members: names; geographic subdivisions smaller than a state (first 3 ZIP digits allowed only if the 3-digit area has >20,000 people, otherwise "000"); all elements of dates (except year) directly related to the individual, and ages over 89 (aggregate as "90 or older"); telephone numbers; fax numbers; email addresses; SSNs; medical record numbers; health plan beneficiary numbers; account numbers; certificate/licence numbers; vehicle identifiers; device identifiers/serial numbers; URLs; IP addresses; biometric identifiers; full-face photographs and comparable images; any other unique identifying number, characteristic or code (except a permitted re-identification code). — [45 CFR 164.514 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.514)
- 164.514(b)(2)(ii): the covered entity must have no **actual knowledge** that the remaining information could identify the individual. — [45 CFR 164.514 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.514)
- 164.514(c): a re-identification code may be retained if not derived from individual information, not used for other purposes, and the mechanism is not disclosed. — [45 CFR 164.514 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.514)
- 164.514(e) **limited data set**: direct identifiers removed (town/city, state, ZIP and dates may remain) and released only under a **data use agreement** that defines permitted uses, who may use/receive, requires safeguards, reporting of unauthorised use, flow-down to agents, and prohibits re-identification or contacting individuals. — [45 CFR 164.514 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.514)
- HHS's de-identification guidance (Nov 2012) explains both methods (no fixed numeric risk threshold is set for Expert Determination; experts commonly use suppression, generalization and perturbation and may time-limit determinations). — [HHS de-identification guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html) [primary, not re-fetched: HTTP 403 this session]

**Small-cell suppression thresholds used by public bodies**
- **CMS (US)**: cell size suppression policy prohibits displaying cells (beneficiaries, procedures, diagnoses) of 1 to 10, i.e., minimum displayed cell size 11; zero is permitted; strategies include collapsing, coarsening, and (complementary) suppression. Older DUAs/2008 notice phrased it as "less than 11"/"10 or less." — [ResDAC: CMS Cell Size Suppression Policy](https://resdac.org/node/1506); [HHS guidance index entry](https://www.hhs.gov/guidance/document/cms-cell-suppression-policy); [archived 2008 CMS notice](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090109183327/http://www.cms.hhs.gov/PrivProtectedData/05_Rerelease.asp) [primary/secondary]
- **NHS England (HES/ECDS)**: if the national total is 1–7, no sub-national breakdown; otherwise sub-national counts of 1–7 shown as "*", zeros unchanged, all other counts rounded to the nearest 5; replaced an older rule that suppressed 1–5 with secondary suppression; applies to counts of individuals. — [NHS Digital HES/ECDS disclosure control methodology](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/data-tools-and-services/data-services/hospital-episode-statistics/disclosure-control-methodology-for-hospital-episode-statistics-and-emergency-care-data-set); see also [DHSC/NHS England disclosure & confidentiality policy (Nov 2018)](https://www.england.nhs.uk/statistics/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2018/12/disclosure_and_confidentiality_nov2018_final.pdf)
- **UK ONS**-based practice is cited by clinical bodies (e.g., RCPCH guidance is "based on the ONS policy on protecting confidentiality in tables of birth and death statistics"). — [RCPCH disclosure-risk guidance](https://www.rcpch.ac.uk/resources/managing-data-disclosure-risks-public-reports-dashboards-rcpch-guidance)
- **CIHI (Canada)**: CIHI's standard defines aggregate data as data aggregated so that individuals "cannot be determined by reasonably foreseeable methods," and notes aggregate data with units of observation **less than 5** may still constitute de-identified (i.e., potentially identifiable) data; research disclosures are de-identified record-level or aggregate data under non-disclosure agreements. Governing policy: *Privacy Policy on the Collection, Use, Disclosure and Retention of PHI and De-Identified Data, 2010* (updated). — [CIHI Health Data Collection Standard](https://repertoiredesindicateurs.icis.ca:443/sites/default/files/document/standard-health-data-collection-en.pdf); [CIHI Health Workforce privacy policy 2011](https://www.cihi.ca/sites/default/files/document/hw_privacy_policy_2011_en.pdf); [CIHI privacy & security page](https://indicatorlibrary.cihi.ca:443/en/about-cihi/privacy-and-security). Canadian thresholds vary by jurisdiction (e.g., Manitoba conventionally suppresses cells <5). — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7501321)
- Best-practice guidance on managing disclosure of de-identified health information in Canada (k-anonymity/risk-threshold approach) is collected in the 2011 "Best Practice Guidelines" (Canadian Institute for Health Information / Privacy Analytics). — [2011 Best Practice Guidelines (PDF)](https://ehealthinformation.ca/web/default/files/wp-files/2011-Best-Practice-Guidelines-for-Managing-the-Disclosure-of-De-Identificatied-Health-Info.pdf) [not fetched]

**UK ICO anonymisation and pseudonymisation guidance (final, 28 March 2025)**
- Final guidance published 28 March 2025 (replacing the 2012 Anonymisation Code of Practice); not a statutory code, but the ICO will take it into account in investigations. — [ICO: About this guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/about-this-guidance/); [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/ico-anonymisation-guide-uk-data-protection-compliance); [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/global-data-hub/2025/spotlight-on-the-uk-data-landscape/gdh---new-ico-guidance-on-anonymisation-and-pseudonymisation)
- Identifiability is a spectrum, context-specific and can change over time; assess "means reasonably likely to be used"; full elimination of risk not required, but risk must be reduced to a sufficiently remote level. — [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/ico-anonymisation-guide-uk-data-protection-compliance) [secondary]
- **Motivated intruder test**: assume a reasonably competent intruder with access to the internet, libraries and public documents; the 2025 guidance adds **generative AI tools** to the "obvious sources" a motivated intruder may use. — [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/ico-anonymisation-guide-uk-data-protection-compliance); [SCL](https://www.scl.org/ico-publishes-anonymisation-guidance/) [secondary]
- Pseudonymised data remain personal data; common techniques are hashing, encryption and tokenisation. — [Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/global-data-hub/2025/spotlight-on-the-uk-data-landscape/gdh---new-ico-guidance-on-anonymisation-and-pseudonymisation) [secondary]

**EU Article 29 Working Party Opinion 05/2014 on Anonymisation Techniques (WP216, adopted 10 April 2014) [primary, not re-fetched]**
- Anonymisation must be robust against three risks: **singling out** (isolating records identifying an individual), **linkability** (linking records relating to the same individual across datasets) and **inference** (deducing an attribute's value with significant probability). Evaluates randomization (noise addition, permutation, differential privacy) and generalization (aggregation/k-anonymity, l-diversity, t-closeness); states pseudonymisation is not an anonymisation method because it only reduces linkability. — [WP216 PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2014/wp216_en.pdf)
- **Successor (draft): EDPB Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation**, adopted for consultation 7 July 2026, consultation closed 30 October 2026 (final not yet adopted as of research date). Proposes three criteria: **no record isolation, no linkage, no inference** (no "specific and meaningful" inference beyond population-level knowledge); data meeting all three are presumed anonymous. Anonymity is relative to each relevant entity using means "reasonably likely" to be used (consistent with *EDPS v SRB*); two approaches—**contextual** (per-recipient) and **simplified** (treat as personal unless anonymous for everyone); lack of motive to re-identify is not reliable; contractual bans complement but do not equal legal prohibitions; anonymisation is itself processing; document the process and reassess periodically (triggers include incidents and advances in AI, especially agentic AI); inferences from querying AI models or synthetic data can breach the inference criterion. — [EDPB consultation page](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/public-consultations/guidelines-022026-on-anonymisation); [Stephenson Harwood](https://www.stephensonharwood.com/insights/the-edpbs-guidelines-on-anonymisation-one-legal-standard-two-approaches-three-criteria/); [DLA Piper](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/08/eu-edpb-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-anonymisation/)
- **EDPB Guidelines 01/2025 on Pseudonymisation** adopted for consultation 16 January 2025 (pseudonymised data remain personal data for controllers holding/able to access the additional information). — [EDPB news](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/news/2025/edpb-adopts-pseudonymisation-guidelines-and-paves-way-improve-cooperation_en); [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/-what-s-in-a-name-edpb-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-pseudonymization)
- **CJEU, C-413/23 P, EDPS v SRB (4 Sept 2025)**: rejected an absolute view that pseudonymised data are always personal data for everyone; identifiability is assessed in context; but the disclosing controller's obligations (e.g., transparency about recipients) are assessed from the controller's perspective at collection, so pseudonymising before transfer does not relieve the discloser. — [Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/09/pseudonymized-data-after-edps-v-srb.html); [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/11/in-a-landmark-decision-eu-court-clarifies); [Covington Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/eu-data-protection/eu-court-of-justice-clarifies-the-concept-of-personal-data-in-the-context-of-a-transfer-of-pseudonymized-data-to-third-parties/) [secondary; commentators differ on how "split" the outcome was]

### Inferences
- Practical tiering a Qatar policy can benchmark:
  - **Ministry (aggregate statistics)**: small-cell rule; a defensible conservative choice is CMS-style "suppress 1–10" (minimum 11) or NHS-style "suppress 1–7 and round to nearest 5," with secondary suppression to prevent differencing. A "<5" rule (Canadian practice) is the least conservative of the cited benchmarks.
  - **Insurers**: claims require identifiable data for payment (a different legal basis); any analytics/secondary use should receive de-identified or aggregate data under a data-use agreement modeled on HIPAA 164.514(e)(4) contents.
  - **External AI vendor**: record-level data are likely pseudonymised rather than anonymous; treat them as personal data on the hospital side (ICO, EDPB, *SRB*), apply a documented risk-based (Expert-Determination-style / motivated-intruder) assessment, keep the key in Qatar, and contractually prohibit re-identification and linkage.
- For free-text clinical notes (relevant to the project), Safe Harbor's identifier list is a useful checklist for named-entity removal, but residual quasi-identifiers in narrative text mean a risk-based assessment (not list-removal alone) is the stronger benchmark.
- Small-population caution: Qatar's population (~3 million, with small nationality/age subgroups) means 3-digit-geography and rare-diagnosis equivalents can be highly identifying; thresholds calibrated to the US/UK may need to be stricter.

### Gaps
- Exact current CIHI mandatory suppression rule (the "<5" figure is confirmed only as a definitional statement in CIHI standards, not as a published hard rule).
- Numeric re-identification risk thresholds used by regulators (e.g., EMA Policy 0070 / Health Canada's 0.09 threshold for clinical reports) are commonly cited but were not verified this session.
- Whether EDPB Guidelines 01/2025 (pseudonymisation) have been finalized was not confirmed.
- ONS's own statistical disclosure control policy text was not retrieved.

## 4. GDPR as comparator (Articles 9, 22, 35, 89; recital 26)

### Takeaway
GDPR is the most-copied model in GCC laws: health data is a **special category** (Art 9) processed only under listed exceptions; scientific research gets derogations conditioned on **safeguards incl. pseudonymisation and data minimisation** (Art 89); solely automated decisions with significant effects are restricted and require human intervention rights (Art 22); high-risk processing (large-scale health data, new technologies like AI) needs a **DPIA** (Art 35); and **anonymous data fall outside GDPR** if identification is not reasonably likely (recital 26).

### Cited Findings
All from Regulation (EU) 2016/679 — [GDPR on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) [primary, not re-fetched; paraphrased]
- **Art 4(1)** personal data; **Art 4(5)** pseudonymisation = processing so data can no longer be attributed to a specific person without additional information kept separately under technical and organisational measures; **Art 4(15)** data concerning health. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **Art 9(1)** prohibits processing health data unless an Art 9(2) exception applies, notably (a) explicit consent; (h) preventive/occupational medicine, medical diagnosis, provision of health or social care or management of health systems, under professional secrecy (Art 9(3)); (i) public health; (j) archiving, scientific/historical research or statistics in accordance with Art 89(1). **Art 9(4)**: Member States may maintain or introduce further conditions for genetic, biometric or health data. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **Art 22(1)**: right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, producing legal or similarly significant effects; **22(2)** exceptions (contract, law, explicit consent); **22(3)** safeguards incl. right to human intervention, to express a view and to contest; **22(4)** such decisions must not be based on special-category data unless Art 9(2)(a) or (g) applies with suitable safeguards. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **Art 35(1)**: DPIA required where processing, particularly using new technologies, is likely to result in high risk; **35(3)(b)** explicitly covers large-scale processing of special categories; **35(7)** minimum content (description, necessity/proportionality, risks, mitigating measures); **Art 36** prior consultation if high residual risk. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **Art 89(1)**: research/statistics processing subject to appropriate safeguards ensuring technical and organisational measures, in particular data minimisation, which may include pseudonymisation; where purposes can be fulfilled by processing that does not permit identification, they shall be fulfilled that way. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **Recital 26**: pseudonymised data that could be attributed with additional information are personal data; to determine identifiability account should be taken of all means reasonably likely to be used (e.g., singling out), considering objective factors such as cost, time and available technology; the principles do not apply to anonymous information. — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- Related articles a policy usually cites alongside: **Art 25** data protection by design/default; **Art 28** processor contracts; **Art 32** security (incl. pseudonymisation and encryption); **Arts 44–49** international transfers (Art 46 SCCs). — [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- The EU "Digital Omnibus" proposal to codify the *SRB* relative approach into the GDPR definition of personal data was opposed by the EDPB (status of that GDPR amendment not confirmed). — [Stephenson Harwood](https://www.stephensonharwood.com/insights/the-edpbs-guidelines-on-anonymisation-one-legal-standard-two-approaches-three-criteria/)

### Inferences
- A Qatar policy can mirror GDPR's structure: (i) a lawful-basis matrix per recipient (care/insurer payment = Art 9(2)(h)-type basis; Ministry = public health/legal obligation, 9(2)(i); AI vendor development/validation = research or explicit consent with Art 89-style safeguards); (ii) a mandatory DPIA before sharing with the AI tool (large-scale health data + new technology = Art 35(3)(b) trigger); (iii) an Art 22-style rule that the AI tool's output is advisory and every diagnosis is confirmed by a clinician.
- Recital 26 + WP29/EDPB tests provide the standard for when the hospital may treat a dataset as outside data-protection rules; absent that, treat shared data as personal.

### Gaps
- Status (adopted or not) of any Digital Omnibus amendments to the GDPR itself as of Nov 2026 was not verified (only the AI Act omnibus was confirmed).
- The EU **European Health Data Space Regulation (EU) 2025/327** (secondary-use permits, health data access bodies, anonymised-by-default release) is a highly relevant comparator but was not researched this session.

## 5. AI-specific frameworks (WHO 2021 and LMM guidance, EU AI Act Arts 10 and 14, NIST AI RMF, OECD AI Principles)

### Takeaway
WHO supplies the health-specific ethical principles (6 principles, 2021) and generative-AI/LMM governance recommendations (Jan 2024, 40+ recommendations); the **EU AI Act (Reg. (EU) 2024/1689)** is the most concrete legal benchmark: diagnostic AI that is a medical device requiring notified-body assessment is **high-risk** (Art 6(1) + Annex I), triggering data-governance (Art 10), transparency (Art 13), human-oversight (Art 14) and deployer duties (Art 26). The **Digital Omnibus on AI (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026)** postponed high-risk application to **2 Dec 2027 (Annex III)** and **2 Aug 2028 (Annex I, incl. medical devices)**, superseding the original 2 Aug 2026/2027 dates. **NIST AI RMF 1.0** (Govern/Map/Measure/Manage) and the **OECD AI Principles (2019, revised 2024)** provide the voluntary risk-management and values layer.

### Cited Findings
**WHO**
- *Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health* (28 June 2021): first global report, produced after two years of expert consultation; six guiding principles: protecting human autonomy; promoting human well-being, safety and the public interest; ensuring transparency, explainability and intelligibility; fostering responsibility and accountability; ensuring inclusiveness and equity; promoting AI that is responsive and sustainable. — [WHO news release](https://who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use); [WHO publication](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)
- *Ethics and governance of AI for health: Guidance on large multi-modal models* (18 January 2024): 40+ recommendations to governments, developers and providers; e.g., assign a regulatory agency to assess/approve LMMs for health use; mandatory post-release auditing and impact assessments (incl. data protection and human rights) when deployed at scale; flags risks of false, inaccurate, biased or incomplete outputs and automation bias. — [WHO publication](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240084759); [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-01-2024-who-releases-ai-ethics-and-governance-guidance-for-large-multi-modal-models)
- *Regulatory considerations on AI for health* (19 October 2023): six areas incl. documentation and transparency, risk management/lifecycle approach, intended use and analytical/clinical validation, data quality, privacy and data protection, and engagement/collaboration; cites GDPR and HIPAA as frameworks developers must navigate. — [WHO news release](https://who.int/news/item/19-10-2023-who-outlines-considerations-for-regulation-of-artificial-intelligence-for-health); [Baker McKenzie summary](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/key-considerations-for-regulation-of-artificial-intelligence-for-health/)

**EU AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689** — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) [primary, not re-fetched; paraphrased]
- **Art 6(1) + Annex I Section A**: an AI system is high-risk where it is (or is a safety component of) a product covered by listed EU harmonisation legislation and must undergo third-party conformity assessment; Annex I includes the Medical Devices Regulation (EU) 2017/745 and IVD Regulation (EU) 2017/746, so most diagnostic AI software is high-risk. — [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **Art 10 (data and data governance)**: training/validation/testing data subject to governance practices covering design choices, data collection and origin (and original purpose for personal data), preparation (annotation, labelling, cleaning), examination for possible biases and measures to detect/prevent/mitigate them, and identification of data gaps (10(2)); data must be relevant, sufficiently representative and, to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete (10(3)); must account for the specific geographical, contextual, behavioural or functional setting of intended use (10(4)); exceptional processing of special-category data for bias detection with strict safeguards (10(5)). — [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **Art 14 (human oversight)**: high-risk AI must be designed so natural persons can effectively oversee it; overseers must be enabled to understand capacities and limitations, remain aware of **automation bias**, correctly interpret outputs, decide not to use or to disregard/override/reverse output, and intervene or stop the system (14(4)). — [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **Art 13** transparency/instructions for use; **Art 26** deployer duties: use per instructions, assign human oversight to persons with competence, training and authority, ensure input data under deployer control are relevant and sufficiently representative, monitor and report risks/incidents, keep automatically generated logs for at least six months, and use Art 13 information when doing a GDPR DPIA. — [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**: published in the OJ 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026; high-risk obligations deferred to **2 December 2027** for Annex III stand-alone systems and **2 August 2028** for Annex I products such as medical devices; Commission empowered to limit duplicative AI Act requirements where sectoral law (e.g., MDR) already imposes equivalent obligations; permission to process special-category data for bias detection extended to providers/deployers of all AI systems, under a strict-necessity standard; Art 4 AI literacy recast as a duty to take measures supporting AI literacy (no fixed individual level). Art 50 transparency duties still apply from 2 Aug 2026. — [EUR-Lex 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj?locale=en); [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/eu-ai-act-unpacked-34-the-final-digital-omnibus-on-ai-key-amendments-to-the-a-102nber); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/eu-ai-omnibus-enters-force-amending-ai-act); [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/07/27/the-digital-omnibus-on-ai-enters-into-force-today-102nedo); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)
- Superseded: original AI Act dates of 2 Aug 2026 (Annex III) and 2 Aug 2027 (Annex I). — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)

**NIST AI Risk Management Framework**
- NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1, January 2023): four functions GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE; trustworthiness characteristics include valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed; companion Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024). NIST states AI RMF 1.0 is **under revision** (no revised version published as of the research date). — [NIST AI RMF page](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework); [AIRC knowledge base](https://airc.nist.gov/AI_RMF_Knowledge_Base)
- NIST also updated its Privacy Framework (draft 1.1, 2025) to address AI. — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/05/nist-updates-its-privacy-framework-to-address-ai)

**OECD AI Principles** [primary, not re-fetched]
- OECD Recommendation on AI (OECD/LEGAL/0449), adopted 2019 and revised May 2024; five values-based principles: inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being; respect for rule of law, human rights and democratic values incl. fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; accountability. — [OECD.AI principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles); [OECD legal instrument](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/en/instruments/OECD-LEGAL-0449)

### Inferences
- For the external AI diagnostic tool, a Qatar policy can benchmark: (1) WHO 2021 principles as the ethical preamble; (2) AI Act Art 10 to require the vendor to evidence that training/validation data are representative of the **Qatari/Gulf patient population** (Art 10(4) setting-specific) and bias-tested; (3) AI Act Art 14 + Art 26 to require named, trained clinician overseers, override capability, logging (≥6 months) and incident reporting; (4) NIST AI RMF functions to structure the hospital's AI risk register; (5) ISO/IEC 42001/42005 for certifiable process evidence.
- Because the EU high-risk dates now fall in Dec 2027/Aug 2028, vendors selling into the EU may not yet hold AI-Act conformity; CE marking under MDR remains the operative evidence until then.
- WHO LMM guidance is relevant if the "AI diagnostic tool" uses a generative/LMM component (e.g., summarising clinical notes).

### Gaps
- No WHO 2025 update to the LMM guidance was found; if one exists it was not located.
- Exact amended article numbering after the Digital Omnibus (e.g., whether Art 10(5) was moved to a new article) and any amendments to Art 14 were not confirmed.
- UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) and the Council of Europe AI Framework Convention (2024) were not researched.

## 6. GCC comparators: Saudi PDPL, UAE Health Data Law (Federal Law No. 2 of 2019), UAE PDPL, Bahrain PDPL

### Takeaway
GCC neighbours split into two models: **sector-specific health-data localisation (UAE)**, where Federal Law No. 2 of 2019 Art 13 bars storing/processing/transferring UAE health data abroad except under Ministerial Resolution 51/2021's ten exceptions (incl. research, insurance) or authority approval; and **general PDPL regimes (Saudi, Bahrain, UAE PDPL)** that classify health data as sensitive and regulate cross-border transfers via adequacy, contracts or permits rather than blanket localisation. Saudi Arabia's SDAIA guideline expressly treats properly anonymised data as no longer personal data and requires irreversibility and periodic review.

### Cited Findings
**Saudi Arabia — PDPL (Royal Decree M/19 of 2021, amended 2023; in force 14 Sept 2023, enforcement from Sept 2024)**
- **PDPL Art 23** (health data): implementing regulations must set additional controls for health data; strict access controls, processing limited to what is needed for health services or health insurance, and access restricted to the minimum number of staff. Detailed duties appear in the **Implementing Regulation** (health-data article reported as Art 26: document all processing stages and the person responsible for each, bind processors by contract, apply MoH/health-regulator controls). Article numbering differs between secondary sources. — [SDAIA Implementing Regulation (PDF)](https://sdaia.gov.sa/en/SDAIA/about/Documents/ImplementingRegulation.pdf); [ksapdpl.com Art 23 commentary](https://ksapdpl.com/ksa-saudi-pdpl-article-23-special-rules-for-health-data-processing/) [secondary]
- **Anonymisation**: SDAIA's *Personal Data Destruction, Anonymisation, and Pseudonymisation Guideline* (3 Sept 2024) defines anonymisation as removing direct and indirect identifiers; anonymised data are no longer personal data; controllers must ensure anonymisation is irreversible (re-identification impossible) and regularly review techniques against emerging risks. — [SDAIA guideline (PDF)](https://sdaia.gov.sa/Documents/PersonalDataDestructionAnonymizationAndEncryptionGuideline.pdf); [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/change/10893)
- **Cross-border transfers**: governed by the *Regulation on Personal Data Transfer Outside the Kingdom* (updated version in force 1 Sept 2024): adequacy, Saudi SCCs, binding common rules, certification; SDAIA risk-assessment guideline for transfers issued Feb 2025; one source reports the adequacy list still pending as of June 2026; stricter sector rules prevail. — [HFW](https://www.hfw.com/insights/cross-border-data-transfers-in-ksa-standard-contractual-clauses-vs-binding-common-rules/); [Clyde & Co](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2025/03/update-on-saudi-arabia-risk-assessment-guidelines); [SGC Consulting](https://www.sgc.consulting/sdaia-saudi-personal-data-protection-law-pdpl-compliance-guide/) [secondary]
- **PDPL Art 27 (research/statistics without consent)**: permitted where (1) the data do not identify a specific individual; (2) identifying evidence is destroyed during processing and before disclosure to another party, and the data are not sensitive; or (3) another law requires it or a prior agreement with the data subject provides for it; the Implementing Regulation adds controls. — [Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/2023-11/saudi-arabias-personal-data-protection-law-%E2%80%93-what-you-need-to-know); [Mondaq overview](https://www.mondaq.com/saudiarabia/data-protection/1641982/saudi-arabias-personal-data-protection-law-a-comprehensive-overview-of-the-legal-and-regulatory-framework) [secondary]
- **PDPL Art 29 (transfers outside the Kingdom)**: only for listed purposes (e.g., obligations under agreements the Kingdom is party to, serving the Kingdom's interests, obligations to which the data subject is party), to destinations with adequate protection or under appropriate safeguards, limited to the minimum data necessary and, where required, after a documented risk assessment; elaborated by the Transfer Regulation (updated 1 Sept 2024). — [King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/insights/articles/international-personal-data-transfers-under-saudi-arabias-data-protection-law); [Securiti](https://securiti.ai/regulation-on-personal-data-transfer-outside-the-kingdom) [secondary]
- PwC notes a transfer exception for vital interests or the prevention, examination or treatment of disease, subject to limits. — [PwC PDPL series Part 3](https://www.pwc.com/m1/en/blogs/pdf/ksa-personal-data-protection-law-series-part-3.pdf) [secondary]
- No default PDPL localisation rule was found; cloud/CST rules may affect hosting of health data. — [recordinglaw.com](https://www.recordinglaw.com/world-laws/world-data-privacy-laws/saudi-arabia-data-privacy-laws/data-transfers-and-localization/); [ScienceDirect review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S219985312600096X) [secondary]
- SDAIA and the Ministry of Health signed an MoU (Oct 2024) on managing national health data; MoH data-sharing requests must comply with NDMO and National Cybersecurity Authority controls. — [SPA](https://www.spa.gov.sa/en/N2193560); [MoH Health Data Portal: data sharing](https://hdp.moh.gov.sa/data-sharing)

**UAE — Federal Law No. 2 of 2019 on the Use of ICT in Health Fields ("Health Data Law")**
- **Art 13**: health data relating to health services provided in the UAE may not be stored, processed, generated or transferred outside the State except in cases specified by a decision of the health authority in coordination with the Ministry (MOHAP). — [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=6854a690-61f4-460b-9a66-bfad5eab127c); [Nabady Whisper](https://nabadywhisper.com/en/blog/uae-health-data-law-federal-law-2-2019-localisation) [secondary]
- **Ministerial Resolution No. 51 of 2021** (issued 28 April 2021; reported effective 16 May 2021) lists **ten** permitted exceptions, reported to include treatment abroad, overseas laboratory testing, approved scientific research, insurance claims, cooperation with UAE government bodies, simple wearables, pharmacovigilance, telemedicine, a formal patient request, and other approved transfers; it confirms the law applies to health data of UAE patients relating to services provided in the UAE. Conditions per exception vary across commentators (e.g., consent, encryption, retaining a copy in the UAE). — [Global Compliance News](https://www.globalcompliancenews.com/2021/07/21/united-arab-emirates-health-data-law-permitted-transfers-of-health-data-06072021/); [Lexology (permitted transfers)](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=d84f230d-fef9-4ff3-a83e-a11fdc79dbf6) [secondary]
- **UAE PDPL (Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021), Art 2**: excludes health personal data that is subject to legislation regulating its protection and processing; the PDPL itself sets transfer conditions rather than a storage mandate; health localisation comes from Federal Law No. 2 of 2019. — [PDPL English text (Lexis/Privacy Arabia PDF)](https://privacyarabia.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Decree-Law-45-2021-Data-Protection-Law-English.pdf); [Securiti](https://securiti.ai/uae-personal-data-protection-law/); [iConnect](https://www.iconnectitbs.com/data-residency-in-the-uae/) [primary text located, not fetched; secondary]

**Bahrain — Law No. 30 of 2018 (PDPL)**
- Health data are treated as sensitive personal data requiring explicit consent or a statutory exception (e.g., public health). Transfers abroad are restricted under **Arts 12 and 13**: permitted to jurisdictions on the Authority's adequacy list (**Resolution No. 42 of 2022**) without permission; otherwise generally requires Authority permission/derogations; breaching transfer rules can be a criminal offence. The law does not define health data or pseudonymisation. — [DLA Piper Data Protection (Bahrain)](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?c=BH&t=law); [Clyde & Co 2022](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2022/04/bahrain-issues-new-privacy-guidelines); [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update-articles/how-does-bahrains-new-personal-data-protection-law-impact-patient-privacy); [Law text (LLOC, .docx)](https://www.lloc.gov.bh/FullEn/K3018.docx) [secondary; law text not fetched]

### Inferences
- If the external AI tool is cloud-hosted outside Qatar, the UAE model (default localisation + enumerated exceptions + regulator approval) is the strictest regional benchmark; the Saudi/Bahrain model (adequacy/contract/permit + risk assessment) is the more permissive one. A Qatar policy can adopt a hybrid: identifiable data stay in Qatar; only de-identified data leave, and only to approved recipients under contract and transfer risk assessment.
- Saudi Arabia's explicit statement that properly anonymised data are not personal data (with an irreversibility standard and periodic review) is the closest GCC precedent for a "release only anonymised data to the AI vendor" rule. Saudi PDPL Art 27 adds a useful nuance: the "destroy identifiers before disclosure" research route does **not** extend to sensitive data such as health data, so health research generally needs a different basis.
- UAE Resolution 51/2021 explicitly recognises research and insurance as permitted cross-border cases, which maps to two of the three recipients in this scenario.

### Gaps
- Saudi PDPL Art 27 and Art 29 content above comes from law-firm summaries; the official statute text was not quoted directly. A reported 17 Feb 2026 SDAIA rule on transfer certification (requiring registration on the National Register of Controllers) was not verified.
- Exact UAE Health Data Law articles on confidentiality exceptions (e.g., disclosure to insurers) and minimum retention period were not verified; whether de-identified/anonymised health data fall outside Art 13 is not addressed in the sources found.
- No source confirmed how Bahrain's PDPA treats anonymised health data or whether a Bahraini health-sector data rule exists.
- Oman (Royal Decree 6/2022 PDPL) and Kuwait were not covered.
