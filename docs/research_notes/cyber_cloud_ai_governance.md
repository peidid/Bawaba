# Qatar Cybersecurity, Cloud/Data-Residency and AI Governance Rules Relevant to a Hospital De-identification Gateway Sending Data to an External (Cloud/Foreign) AI Diagnostic Service

Scope note: current to 22 November 2026 as far as sources allowed. "Primary" = document issued by a Qatari authority, or hosted on its site. "Secondary" = law firm, consultancy, vendor or news. Several official portals (mcit.gov.qa, gco.gov.qa, UNODC SHERLOC, Lexology) refused automated fetches (HTTP 403), so some items rest on secondary sources. Those are flagged. Privacy-law (PDPPL) detail is covered by other researchers and appears here only where it interacts with cyber, cloud or AI rules.

## 1. National Information Assurance (NIA) Policy/Standard: current version, classification levels, relevant controls, and whether health providers must comply

### Takeaway
The current baseline is NCSA's **National Information Assurance Standard (NIAS) V2.1**, used with the **National Data Classification Policy (NDCP) V3.0**. Per a Qatari consultancy, both were published in May 2023 and replaced the 2014 ictQATAR/MOTC **NIA Policy V2.0**; recertifications against V2.1 have been mandatory since 1 January 2024. Certification is run by NCSA. Secondary sources describe it as mandatory for government and critical-sector bodies, healthcare included, but I could not retrieve the official applicability clause. I found no confirmed public record of any named Qatari hospital holding NIA certification.

### Cited Findings
**Versions and governance**
- NCSA runs training on "Data Classification in the context of National Data Classification Policy V3.0 and how to secure their information assets using the National Information Assurance Standard V2.1". This confirms that the two current instruments are applied together. — [NCSA news (primary), training course on NDCP and NIAS](https://ncsa.gov.qa/en/media/news/NCSA%20Conducts%20the%20Sixth%20Training%20Course%20on%20National%20Data%20Classification%20Policy%20and%20National%20Information%20Assurance%20Standard%20Implementation-430182)
- NIAS V2.1 and NDCP V3.0 were both published in May 2023 and "together replaced the long-running National Information Assurance Policy V2.0". V2.1 took effect on publication. NDCP V3.0 gave a six-month implementation window. — [Vantage (Doha consultancy, secondary)](https://www.vantage.com.qa/blog/nia-v2-1-vs-v2-0-what-changed)
- The **NIA Certification Scoping Standard V3.2** was published in December 2023 and took effect on 1 January 2024. V2.0 applications closed on 31 December 2023, and there is no V2.0 renewal path. V2.0 certificates (typically 12 months) remain valid for their term. — [Vantage (secondary)](https://www.vantage.com.qa/blog/nia-v2-1-vs-v2-0-what-changed)
- The legacy document is the **ictQATAR "National Information Assurance Policy" V2.0** (English PDF, hosted by BSI, an NCSA-accredited NIA auditor). — [ictQATAR NIA Policy v2.0 PDF (BSI-hosted)](https://www.bsigroup.com/globalassets/localfiles/en-ae/digitaltrust/niap/ictqatar-nia_policy_english-v2.0.pdf); [BSI: accredited by NCSA to conduct NIA audits](https://bsigroup.com/en-AE/Our-services/digital-trust/national-information-assurance-policy-audit) *(the PDF itself was not read in this session; usage limit hit)*
- Governance passed from MoTC to NCSA under Emiri/Amiri Decree No. 1 of 2021. NCSA's **National Cyber Governance and Assurance Affairs (NCGAA)** division owns NIA certification and policy. The NIA Standard is called the cornerstone of the **National Information Security Compliance Framework (NISCF)**. — [Vantage (secondary)](https://www.vantage.com.qa/blog/nia-v2-1-vs-v2-0-what-changed)
- Eversheds Sutherland lists an **NCSA NISCF Audit Standard v3.0 (2024)**, which treats AI only as an audit tool (Section A.C.E.3.2: AI-generated audit results must be accurate, reliable and validated). — [Eversheds Sutherland, "Mapping Qatar's Regulatory Landscape for AI", Nov 2025](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/united-states/insights/mapping-qatars-regulatory-landscape-for-artificial-intelligence-november-2025)

**Classification levels (sources conflict; primary NDCP V3.0 text not retrieved)**
- Vantage's version of NDCP V3.0: **C0** Public/Unclassified (no expected damage); **C1** Restricted (Internal), light-to-moderate damage; **C2** Restricted, limited to defined users/roles, serious damage; **C3** Restricted (Confidential), very small set of named persons, severe damage; **C4+** National Security Markings (Confidential/Secret/Top Secret). It replaced legacy labels "Unclassified, Restricted, Confidential, Secret, Top Secret". Government entities must adopt the full scheme. Non-government organisations must implement "at least a four-tier subset" that maps to it. A Chief Data Officer or equivalent is required. — [Vantage (secondary)](https://www.vantage.com.qa/blog/nia-v2-1-vs-v2-0-what-changed)
- The DCO's 2025 Qatar report says "The National Data Classification Policy establishes five data levels and imposes security and risk assessment obligations by level". This matches a C0 to C4 scheme. — [Digital Cooperation Organization, Digital Trade Acceleration Initiative – Qatar Country Report (Oct 2025), p.28](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)
- **Conflicting descriptions:** NHIMG describes confidentiality, integrity and availability ratings **C0–C3, I0–I3, A0–A3**. At C1 all baseline controls apply, C2 adds at least one control per domain, and C3 adds at least two. This looks like the V2.0/NIAS impact-rating model. — [NHIMG (secondary)](https://nhimg.org/qatar-national-information-assurance-standard-guide). GRCLens says the labels were extended to four tiers (Internal, Restricted, Secret, Top Secret). — [GRCLens (vendor)](https://www.grclens.net/frameworks/qatar-nias). CyberQuess lists Unclassified/Restricted/Confidential/Secret/Top Secret. — [CyberQuess (vendor)](https://cyberquess.ae/ncsa-compliance-services/)
- A government cloud summary says data at C4 (national security) is exempt from cloud migration. — [summary of CRA/Cloud-First materials (secondary, via search)](https://dig.watch/resource/qatars-cloud-first-policy) *(single secondary source; not verified)*

**Control structure relevant to a de-identification gateway**
- There are **26 control domains**: 13 under "Security Governance and Security Processes" and 13 under "Security Technical and Operational Controls". The domains are unchanged in V2.1. V2.1 clarifies the evidence assessors request, including "documentation, logs, and operational evidence". — [Vantage (secondary)](https://www.vantage.com.qa/blog/nia-v2-1-vs-v2-0-what-changed)
- Under Scoping Standard V3.2, applicants must document information flows, "including cross-entity and third-party processing", technology environments, classified asset population and justified exclusions. — [Vantage (secondary)](https://www.vantage.com.qa/blog/nia-v2-1-vs-v2-0-what-changed)
- Certification preparation typically requires a Business Impact Assessment, a Compliance Roadmap, an Information Asset Classification Register and a Statement of Applicability. An NCSA-accredited auditor performs the audit and NCSA makes the award decision. — [search summary of NIA certification guides (secondary): Microsoft Learn, Qatar NIA offering](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/compliance/offerings/offering-qatar-nia); [Security Scientist (secondary)](https://www.securityscientist.net/blog/12-questions-and-answers-about-qatar-nia-policy-regulation/)
- A Qatari GitHub register cross-walks NIAS v2.1 to ISO 27001 and NIST CSF 2.0 and PDPPL obligations. It is a useful practical mapping, not authoritative. — [SiteQ8/Senyar (GitHub, non-official)](https://github.com/SiteQ8/Senyar)

**Who must comply / health sector**
- One secondary source calls NIA a mandatory baseline for government ministries and critical national infrastructure operators, health included, with semi-government bodies mandatory or strongly expected. Another says organisations with information infrastructure critical to Qatar must obtain certification. — [search summary of Microsoft Learn / Kiteworks NIA guides (secondary)](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/compliance/offerings/offering-qatar-nia); [Kiteworks NIA 2.1 guide (vendor)](https://www.kiteworks.com/guide-kiteworks-data-layer-guide-to-qatar-national-information-assurance-standard-compliance/)
- NCSA has said it expects "more government entities and organisations within critical sectors will pursue NIA certification". This wording suggests certification is being rolled out, not that every critical-sector body already holds it. — [The Peninsula, 28 Jan 2026, Milaha NIA compliance](https://s.thepeninsula.qa/article/28/01/2026/qatar-bolsters-cybersecurity-as-ncsa-confirms-milahas-compliance-for-nia)
- **UNVERIFIED:** one search-engine summary claimed NCSA announced that Sidra Medicine was "the first healthcare institution to successfully complete the initial phase of its compliance roadmap under the National NIA Standard". A follow-up search found no source for this. The comparable confirmed case is Milaha, which completed its NIA compliance roadmap and expanded certification organisation-wide in January 2026. Sidra's confirmed certification is **ISO/IEC 27001** for its IT operations (TÜV Rheinland, 2023). Do not cite the Sidra NIA claim. — [The Peninsula, 28 Jan 2026 (Milaha)](https://s.thepeninsula.qa/article/28/01/2026/qatar-bolsters-cybersecurity-as-ncsa-confirms-milahas-compliance-for-nia); [The Peninsula, 11 Feb 2023 (Sidra ISO 27001)](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/11/02/2023/sidra-medicine-achieves-iso27001-certification-for-it-operations)
- Microsoft describes the NIA Policy as established by MCIT (formerly MOTC/ictQATAR). Certification sits within NCSA's National Information Security Compliance Framework. — [Microsoft Learn, Qatar NIA compliance offering](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/compliance/offerings/offering-qatar-nia)
- The first NIA certificate of compliance was issued in May 2022. Microsoft became the first global cloud provider in Qatar to receive NIA certification for its Qatar datacentre region. — [The Peninsula, 17 May 2022](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/17/05/2022/first-national-information-assurance-certificate-of-compliance-issued); [The Peninsula, 4 Oct 2023](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/04/10/2023/ncsa-certifications-reflect-microsofts-efforts-to-provide-trusted-cloud-services-khalaf)
- The CRA Cloud Policy Framework tells government entities to use the NIA Policy for classification: "For the classification of government data, government entities shall refer to the National Information Assurance Policy (NIAP)" (fn.12). It also lists "the industry using the data e.g. defense, national security, health, finance" as a classification factor (s.4.2). — [CRA Cloud Policy Framework, June 2022 (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)

### Inferences
- Large public hospitals (HMC, PHCC) are government entities, and Sidra is semi-government. For them, NIAS V2.1 and NDCP V3.0 should be treated as the governing security baseline. Identifiable patient data would most plausibly sit at **C2 or C3** (serious or severe damage), but no source states the health mapping. An NIA-certified scope would need to show the gateway's flow to the external AI vendor as a documented "third-party processing" information flow.
- The gateway should produce the evidence NIAS assessors expect: access logs, transformation logs, and records of what left the boundary. Its de-identified output should be classified separately and justified (for example, a lower C-level only if re-identification risk is controlled).
- A foreign AI vendor that is not NIA-certified would be a supplier risk the hospital must manage contractually. NIA certification of the hyperscaler hosting the service (e.g. Microsoft's Qatar region) is a relevant assurance signal.

### Gaps
- I did not read the primary NDCP V3.0 or NIAS V2.1 text. NCSA library pages and the PDFs were not retrieved, and the usage limit was hit before the NIA Policy v2.0 PDF could be read. So the exact C-level names, the health-data examples, the domain names (e.g. "Third Party Security", "Logging and Security Monitoring", "Data Labelling", "Cloud Security"), log-retention periods and the official applicability clause are unverified.
- No source names the NIA certification status of Hamad Medical Corporation, PHCC or Sidra.

## 2. Cloud and data localisation: cloud policy, NCSA/CRA standards, health/sensitive data residency, PDPPL cross-border interaction, MoPH hosting rules

### Takeaway
No primary source found makes **clinical** health data residency in Qatar a general legal requirement. The one explicit health-sector residency rule found is a **MoPH QNHI Data Management directive (Dec 2022)**. Citing Minister of Public Health Decision No. (8) of 2022 (executive regulations of Law No. (22) of 2021 on healthcare services), it requires that **health insurance data** be stored "in electronic systems or cloud infrastructure hosted inside the state of Qatar". The CRA **Cloud Policy Framework (June 2022)** is expressly anti-localisation. It says sensitive data "shall not" be prevented from cloud storage "whether local or abroad", and it limits localisation to "highly sensitive data only". Two things pull the other way. Government data falls under the MCIT **Cloud-First Policy** (endorsed CSPs that meet residency requirements) and the NDCP. And a January 2026 PwC presentation hosted by NCSA reports a "growing requirement to store personal data with special nature [which includes health] within Qatar on local or an approved cloud infrastructure". Beyond the insurance-data directive, I found no primary MoPH rule on hosting clinical records abroad.

### Cited Findings
**CRA Cloud Policy Framework (June 2022, primary; policy recommendations, not a statute)**
- The CRA presents the framework as a strategic action identifying "key areas where legal and regulatory review is needed". CRA's mandate comes from Emiri Decree No. (42) of 2014. — [CRA Cloud Policy Framework (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)
- s.4.1 Cloud-First: "Security is not any more a primary inhibitor to the adoption of cloud services. Accordingly, the sensitive nature of data shall not prevent storage on the cloud (whether local or abroad)." MCIT "has developed a Cloud-First Policy focused on procurement processes". Recommendation: "Qatar shall implement a cloud-first policy for public procurement of cloud services by government entities… Cloud solutions shall be assessed before any on-premise solutions and based on a clear data classification policy." — [CRA Cloud Policy Framework (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)
- s.4.2: "control on encryption (at least the mechanism whereby the encryption keys are stored and managed) should lie with the owner of the information for government classified data". — [CRA (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)
- s.4.3.1: imposing local storage "would be counter-productive". For the most sensitive categories, organisations should use measures "more efficient than localization requirements", such as **encryption, anonymization, aggregation and/or storage in pre-defined hubs**, and third-party-audited security certification. "Any request for data localization should be very limited in volume and scope and, address only trade secrets or national security requirements for which no alternative exists." — [CRA (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)
- Policy Recommendation box (s.4.3): "Data residency shall not be any longer a requirement as data classification schemes, security and encryption technologies now secure a high level of protection controls. Data localization may be required for highly sensitive data only… limited in scope and volume… Private cloud solutions shall be chosen to guarantee security and data protection." — [CRA (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)
- s.4.3.2 Free flow of data: cross-border transfer mechanisms include contracts, international standards, binding corporate rules, APEC-CBPR-style rules, certifications and seals, and bilateral or multilateral arrangements. "For personal data, it is recommended that the data subject's prior consent be required", with consent interpreted per Law No. 13 of 2016, Art. 15 (fn.18). There should be "no requirement that the above categories of cross-border transfers be notified to or approved by the relevant authorities". — [CRA (primary)](https://www.cra.gov.qa/-/media/System/A/B/3/F/AB3F01A7D2955C198BC8C1650FFDC104/Cloud-Policy-Framework.ashx)

**Government data, Cloud-First and sector localisation**
- "Qatar does not demand general data localisation, but maintains localisation requirements for government data and in the financial sector." The MCIT Cloud First Policy "recommends the use of 'endorsed cloud service providers,' which have passed Qatar's data residency, security and operational requirements". "Certain government data is subject to residency requirements." The report also says that under the NDCP and the CRA framework "data residency is no longer required". — [DCO Qatar Country Report, Oct 2025, pp.14–15](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)
- QCB Cloud Computing Regulation (financial sector example): QCB-licensed entities must ensure "personally identifiable information and financial information are processed within Qatar" and need QCB approval before cloud arrangements. — [DCO Qatar Country Report, p.15](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf); see also [Eversheds Sutherland, Nov 2025](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/united-states/insights/mapping-qatars-regulatory-landscape-for-artificial-intelligence-november-2025) (which describes the QCB Cloud Computing Regulation (2024) as requiring a cloud register, risk assessments, contractual safeguards and consent before subcontracting, without mentioning residency; the two sources partly conflict)
- Hyperscaler presence: Microsoft Azure has had a Qatar region since 2022 and Google Cloud a Doha region since 2023. — [search summary of vendor blog (secondary)](https://devopsqatar.com/blog/cloud-infrastructure-qatar-national-data-strategy/). Computer Weekly reports Qatar advancing a "sovereign cloud strategy". — [Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366639040/Qatar-advances-sovereign-cloud-strategy-to-strengthen-digital-trust-and-national-autonomy) *(article not opened)*

**Health / special-nature data and PDPPL interaction**
- A presentation hosted on NCSA's site ("Data Privacy in Medical Research", NCSA/PwC, January 2026, presenter Marea O'Toole, PwC) lists among "Key privacy updates in Qatar for 2026": "**Data localization: Growing requirement to store personal data with special nature within Qatar on local or an approved cloud infrastructure.**" It also lists "Mandatory DPIAs… fines up to QAR 1 million" and "AI and open data oversight: New rules… focus on compliant AI use and updated Open Data Policy (v2.0.0)". — [NCSA-hosted PwC deck, IDPD 2026, slide 3](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10207/1.0~fc4efdb1-0710-4db9-a751-c48e068c9509?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Data+Privacy+in+Medical+Research+-+IDPD+2026+%282%29.pdf)
- Same deck: health is "personal data with special nature" under PDPPL Art. 16 and "may only be processed after obtaining the permission from the PDPPD" (NCSA's Personal Data Privacy Protection Department). Cross-border sharing of research data should comply with PDPPD rules and not "cause serious damage to personal data or privacy", "backed by a documented Data Protection Impact Assessment and PDPPD-compliant contractual safeguards" (e.g. SCCs and data-sharing agreements). It prefers anonymisation ("effectively irreversible") and otherwise pseudonymisation "so that only the originating institution can reconnect codes to patients". Reusing treatment data "to train a predictive risk model" is secondary processing requiring compatibility checks, ethics approval, minimisation, de-identification and RoPA documentation. — [NCSA-hosted PwC deck, slides 7, 13, 14, 20](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10207/1.0~fc4efdb1-0710-4db9-a751-c48e068c9509?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Data+Privacy+in+Medical+Research+-+IDPD+2026+%282%29.pdf)
- PDPPL cross-border baseline (summarised by DCO): data controllers "are not allowed to restrict cross-border data flows, unless such processing contravenes the provisions of the law or causes significant harm to the data subject". — [DCO Qatar Country Report](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)
- Eversheds lists an **NCSA PDPPL Guidance Hub (2025)** covering privacy by design and international data transfers. — [Eversheds Sutherland, Nov 2025](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/united-states/insights/mapping-qatars-regulatory-landscape-for-artificial-intelligence-november-2025)
- **MoPH QNHI Data Management directive (Qatar National Health Insurance, dated December 2022; primary MoPH PDF, text taken from a search-engine extract because a direct fetch was rejected; verify):** "in compliance with the cross regulatory guidelines and the Minister of Public Health Decision No. (8) of 2022 issuing the executive regulations of Law No. (22) of 2021 regulating health care services within the country, all entities storing health insurance data shall do so in electronic systems or cloud infrastructure hosted inside the state of Qatar". Also: "entities are required to get patient consent for all stored protected health information". — [MoPH QHIS, QNHI-DATA-MANAGEMENT-ENG.pdf](https://qhis.moph.gov.qa/documents/76932/124581/QNHI-DATA-MANAGEMENT-ENG.pdf)
- **MoPH, general clinical data:** a vendor guide asserts that the Ministry "requires healthcare providers to store and process patient data within national boundaries", with limited exceptions for cross-border transfers. It warns that multi-region cloud defaults (failover, load balancing) can move data abroad. It cites no specific MoPH instrument for clinical records, so this is **unverified**. — [Kiteworks (vendor)](https://www.kiteworks.com/regulatory-compliance/qatar-healthcare-data-residency-compliance/)
- **NCSA "Cloud Computing Privacy Assessment Tool"** (announced about 2 April 2026 by NCSA's Personal Data Privacy Protection Department): a "practical framework enabling entities to assess their current privacy practices" for personal data in cloud environments (governance, risk, PDPPL compliance readiness). It is for public and private organisations and appears voluntary ("encouraged stakeholders to actively utilise the tool"). The article does not mention residency or an approved-cloud list. — [The Peninsula, 3 Apr 2026](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/03/04/2026/qatar-launches-cloud-privacy-assessment-tool-to-strengthen-data-protection)

### Inferences
- The cyber and cloud layer gives two different answers. Under the CRA framework, sending **properly anonymised/de-identified** data to a foreign cloud AI is the outcome the framework favours: it names anonymisation and encryption as alternatives to localisation. Identifiable or pseudonymised special-nature health data is another matter. There, the PDPPL permission regime, the DPIA and the reported "growing" localisation expectation, plus government-entity Cloud-First and NDCP rules for public hospitals, point toward keeping identifiable data in Qatar or on an "approved"/endorsed cloud. That favours a gateway design where re-identification keys never leave the hospital.
- The CRA framework's reliance on "pre-defined hubs" and owner-held encryption keys supports keeping key custody (and any pseudonymisation lookup table) inside the hospital boundary.
- The QNHI directive shows that MoPH is willing to impose in-country hosting by directive, citing Law 22/2021 regulations. Claims and insurance data passing through a hospital system (e.g. payer identifiers in notes) are expressly covered. A gateway should strip such fields, or keep them on Qatar-hosted infrastructure, before anything goes to a foreign AI service.
- The CRA document is a policy framework. Where it conflicts with the PDPPL or NCSA instruments, the statute and NCSA rules would likely prevail.

### Gaps
- I could not read the MCIT **Cloud First Policy** text (mcit.gov.qa returned 403), so its date, its endorsed-CSP list and its residency criteria by classification level are unverified.
- I found no **NCSA cloud security standard** (e.g. a Cloud Security Policy or CSP security standard) in primary form. Whether NIAS V2.1 contains a cloud domain with residency rules is unverified.
- Apart from the QNHI insurance-data directive (whose full text I could not open), I found no **primary MoPH circular, policy or ministerial decision** requiring general clinical records to be hosted in Qatar or restricting hosting abroad. Minister of Public Health Decision No. 8 of 2022 itself was not read. The PwC/NCSA slide gives no legal citation for the "growing requirement".

## 3. Critical National Information Infrastructure (CNII): is healthcare a designated critical sector, and what follows?

### Takeaway
Qatar's 2014 National Cyber Security Strategy listed **healthcare** among the critical sectors whose information infrastructure must be safeguarded (Objective 1). NCSA leadership still names health as a vital sector. The practical result is NIA certification expectations, incident reporting and NCSA critical-infrastructure programmes ("Cyber Shield"). I found no standalone CNII statute or hospital-specific CNII rulebook.

### Cited Findings
- The 2014 Qatar National Cyber Security Strategy sets "Objective 1: Safeguard the national critical information infrastructure". It lists critical sectors as Energy; Electricity and Water; Finance; Government; **Healthcare**; ICT; Transportation. It defines CII as ICT systems, services and data assets critical to Qatar, using criteria such as service recovery time and public-confidence impact. — [Qatar National Cyber Security Strategy (2014), via National Security Archive](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/3903662/Qatari-Government-Qatar-National-Cyber-Security.pdf) *(older document, 2014; content taken from search-result extracts, PDF not opened)*
- The Fraunhofer CIPedia entry lists Qatar's CII sectors, but its excerpt omits healthcare (incomplete listing). — [Fraunhofer CIPedia](https://websites.fraunhofer.de/CIPedia/index.php/Critical_Information_Infrastructure_Sector)
- The NCSA President said threats include "sabotage and disruption of national infrastructure that can directly affect vital sectors such as energy, health, transportation". — [Oxford Business Group interview with NCSA President Abdulrahman Al Farahid Al Malki](https://oxfordbusinessgroup.com/articles-interviews/digital-protection-abdulrahman-ali-al-farahid-al-malki-president-national-cyber-security-agency-ncsa-on-evolving-cybersecurity-regulations-and-how-emerging-technologies-can-protect-infrastructur-interview/)
- The National Information Assurance Policy and the National ICS Security Standard are cited as key guidance for protecting CII. — [search summary of cyberpolicyportal/strategy materials (secondary)](https://database.cyberpolicyportal.org/en/entity/qmh1daz5hbl?page=14)
- NCSA runs a "Cyber Shield" initiative/workshop for critical-infrastructure leaders (May 2026). — [The Peninsula, 26 May 2026](https://www.thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/26/05/2026/qatar-strengthens-critical-infrastructure-security-with-cyber-shield-initiative) *(article not opened)*
- Qatar's framework requires cybersecurity incidents to be reported to authorities. NCSA, which "reports directly to the Council of Ministers", leads oversight with sectoral bodies. — [DCO Qatar Country Report, p.28](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- A public hospital is plausibly CNII (healthcare sector, government entity). It should therefore expect NIA certification, incident notification to NCSA (Q-CERT functions) and supply-chain scrutiny of external AI services. A gateway that becomes a dependency for clinical decisions would also need availability and resilience controls.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the 2024–2030 Strategy or any NCSA decision formally re-designates sectors, or whether a CNII protection law or decree exists in 2026.
- Incident-reporting timelines and the legal instrument behind mandatory reporting are not identified.

## 4. Cybercrime Prevention Law (Law No. 14 of 2014): offences relevant to unauthorised access, disclosure or processing of personal/health data; penalties

### Takeaway
Law No. 14 of 2014 criminalises unauthorised access to information systems, with doubled penalties when access leads to deletion, disclosure, alteration or capture of data. It also covers interception, data damage and privacy offences. Law No. 11 of 2025 added Art. 8 bis (non-consensual images or video). The exact fines per article could not be verified from a primary text in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Law No. 14 of 2014 promulgating the Cybercrime Prevention Law was issued in September 2014. — [The Peninsula, 16 Sep 2014](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/16/09/2014/law-issued-to-combat-cybercrimes); [Gulf Times](https://www.gulf-times.com/story/418155/Qatar-cracks-down-on-cybercrime-with-new-laws)
- The unofficial translation covers accessing a website, information system or network without authorisation, exceeding authorised access, or remaining after access should end. "The penalty… shall be doubled if such access results in" deleting, disclosing, changing or capturing data (list truncated in extract). — [UNODC SHERLOC, unofficial translation (search extract; full PDF returned 403)](https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/uploads/res/document/the-cybercrime-prevention-law_html/Qatar_Cybercrime_Law_unofficial_translation.pdf)
- It is also an offence to "intercept unrightfully or spy on any electronic data". — [Al Tamimi & Co., "Cyber Crime Prevention Law in Qatar" (search extract)](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update-articles/cyber-crime-prevention-law-in-qatar)
- Penalties as described by law-firm commentary (search extracts, not the statute):
  - Deliberate unauthorised access to any website, information system (including hardware) or network: imprisonment up to **3 years and/or a fine of QAR 500,000**.
  - Access to government websites or systems: up to 3 years and up to QAR 500,000.
  - Both doubled "where data is retrieved, manipulated or any interruptions are caused".
  - A related e-card offence: up to 3 years and QAR 200,000.

  — [Al Tamimi & Co.](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update/october-4/articles/cyber-crime-prevention-law-in-qatar/); [Gulf Times](https://www.gulf-times.com/story/418155/qatar-cracks-down-on-cybercrime-with-new-laws)
- Numbering caveat: in the unofficial Squire Patton Boggs translation (18 Sep 2014), Art. 2 of the *promulgating* law is a transitional provision giving service providers six months to comply. The offences sit in the articles of the attached Cybercrime Prevention Law, so article citations should name which instrument is meant. — [UNODC SHERLOC, unofficial translation (search extract)](https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/uploads/res/document/the-cybercrime-prevention-law_html/Qatar_Cybercrime_Law_unofficial_translation.pdf)
- The DCO says the law covers "unauthorised access, hacking, cyber-stalking, data theft, malware distribution, child exploitation, and cyber-terrorism", obliges ISPs to take preventive measures and cooperate, and is enforced by the MoI **Economic and Cyber Crimes Combating Department** with the Public Prosecution. — [DCO Qatar Country Report, pp.29–30](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)
- **Law No. 11 of 2025** (Official Gazette No. 20 of 2025, 4 Aug 2025) added **Art. 8 bis** on publishing individuals' images or videos without consent, with penalties up to 1 year in prison and/or QAR 100,000. — [Lexis Middle East, 8 Aug 2025](https://www.lexis.ae/2025/08/08/qtr-introduces-prison-terms-and-fines-for-online-privacy-violations/); [The Peninsula, 5 Aug 2025](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/05/08/2025/jail-qr100000-fine-for-violation-of-privacy-in-qatar-official-gazette); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/privacy-protection/1670196/qatar-introduces-new-penalties-for-privacy-violations); also in the [NCSA-hosted PwC deck, slide 3](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10207/1.0~fc4efdb1-0710-4db9-a751-c48e068c9509?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Data+Privacy+in+Medical+Research+-+IDPD+2026+%282%29.pdf)
- A breach can trigger parallel proceedings under the Cybercrime Law and the PDPPL (Law No. 13 of 2016). — [search summary of law-firm commentary (secondary)](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update-articles/cyber-crime-prevention-law-in-qatar)
- Rights groups criticise vague provisions (Arts. 5, 6, 8, 11). — [GC4HR](https://www.gc4hr.org/?p=17099)

### Inferences
- For the gateway, the main criminal exposure is (a) unauthorised access or exceeding authorised access by staff, vendors or attackers, with doubled penalties if patient data is disclosed or copied, and (b) interception of data in transit. A vendor's AI service retaining or reusing hospital data beyond contract could arguably be "exceeding authorised access", but no source tests this.

## 5. National Cyber Security Strategy (2024–2030) and NCSA's mandate

### Takeaway
NCSA was created by **Amiri Decree No. 1 of 2021**, reports to the Council of Ministers, and regulates both cybersecurity (NIA/NISCF certification) and personal-data protection (the PDPPL, through its PDPPD). It launched the **National Cyber Security Strategy 2024–2030** on 17 September 2024 under the Prime Minister's patronage, with five pillars.

### Cited Findings
- "Established by Amiri Decree No. (1) of 2021, the NCSA is mandated to protect personal data privacy, develop regulations, and enforce compliance". NCSA, through the **Personal Data Privacy Protection Department (PDPPD)** (formerly the National Data Privacy Office), is the PDPPL regulator. Fines can reach QAR 5 million. — [NCSA-hosted PwC deck, Jan 2026, slide 3](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10207/1.0~fc4efdb1-0710-4db9-a751-c48e068c9509?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Data+Privacy+in+Medical+Research+-+IDPD+2026+%282%29.pdf)
- NCSA "reports directly to the Council of Ministers". Its National Cyber Governance and Assurance Affairs division handles personal-data protection matters. — [DCO Qatar Country Report, pp.6, 28](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)
- The National Cyber Security Strategy 2024–2030 was launched 17 September 2024 under the patronage of the Prime Minister and Minister of Foreign Affairs. — [QNA, 17 Sep 2024](https://www.qna.org.qa/en/newsbulletins/2024-09/17/0041-under-patronage-of-prime-minister-and-minister-of-foreign-affairs,-national-cyber-security-strategy-2024-2030-launched); [The Peninsula, 18 Sep 2024](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/18/09/2024/national-cyber-security-strategy-2024-2030-launched); [Gulf Times](https://www.gulf-times.com/article/690849/qatar/cyber-security-strategy-2024-2030-launched)
- Five pillars: (1) cybersecurity and resilience of the local ecosystem; (2) legislation, regulation and law enforcement of cyberspace; (3) an innovative, data-driven economy; (4) cyber culture/workforce; (5) international partnerships (the wording of pillar 5 was truncated in extracts). The aim is to position Qatar as a global leader in the secure adoption of emerging technologies, aligned with QNV 2030. — [Qatar Tribune](https://qatar-tribune.com/article/140862/nation/new-strategy-to-serve-as-roadmap-to-address-cyber-challenges); [Digital Watch](https://dig.watch/resource/qatars-national-cyber-security-strategy); [US ITA market intelligence](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/qatars-new-cybersecurity-strategy-2024-2030)
- In October 2026 the NCSA Director General described international cooperation as a key pillar of the strategy. — [QNA, 21 Oct 2026](https://qna.org.qa/en/News-Area/News/2026-10/21/ncsa-director-general-qatar-considers-international-cooperation-key-pillar-of-its-cybersecurity-strategy) *(article not opened; NCSA leadership titles/names differ across sources: "President" Al Malki in 2024 vs a "Director General" in Oct 2026)*
- The previous strategy dates from 2014 (MOTC/ictQATAR era). — [National Security Archive copy](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/3903662/Qatari-Government-Qatar-National-Cyber-Security.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because one agency (NCSA) regulates both the cybersecurity baseline (NIA) and the privacy law (PDPPL), a hospital's NIA scope, DPIA and special-nature processing permission for the AI gateway are likely to be reviewed by the same institution and should be prepared together.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full strategy document (gco.gov.qa returned 403). Its specific initiatives (e.g. on cloud, AI or critical sectors) are unverified.

## 6. Artificial intelligence: National AI Strategy, ethics principles and guidelines, NCSA AI security guidelines, QCB guideline, AI law status (2026), GenAI/LLMs, oversight, transparency, bias, accountability

### Takeaway
As of the latest sources (April 2026), Qatar has **no AI statute in force**. AI governance rests on soft-law instruments, in this order:
1. the **National AI Strategy (2019)**;
2. the **NCSA Guidelines for Secure Adoption and Usage of AI (v1.0, Feb 2024)**, which are voluntary and include a GenAI section;
3. two **MCIT ethics guidelines** ("Principles and Guidelines for Ethical Development and Deployment of AI", with eight principles including ultimate human accountability, plus a companion "Ethical Use" guideline; dated 2024, launched May 2025);
4. a **draft National AI Policy (MCIT, Sept 2025, consultation v1.0.0)**;
5. a sectoral binding example, the **QCB AI Guideline (4 Sept 2024)**.

The Shura Council debated an AI governance motion in April 2026. **Cabinet Decision No. 34 of 2026** created a National Center for AI in MCIT, mandated to develop "policies and regulatory frameworks for responsible and safe AI use" (draft approval had come in September 2025). For health, the only AI-specific instrument found is the non-binding **HBKU/MoPH-advised Research Guidelines for Healthcare AI Development**.

### Cited Findings
**National AI Strategy (2019) and institutions**
- The National Artificial Intelligence Strategy for Qatar (2019) is hosted by MCIT. — [MCIT PDF](https://www.mcit.gov.qa/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2025/02/national_artificial_intelligence_strategy_for_qatar_2019_en.pdf) *(not opened; mcit.gov.qa blocks automated fetch)*
- MCIT's draft policy summarises the 2019 strategy's aims as fostering AI innovation, building a skilled workforce, ensuring access to "computing power, data, and connectivity", and establishing "guidelines for the responsible and ethical use of AI". The Artificial Intelligence Committee (AIC) was set up in 2021 to coordinate implementation. — [MCIT draft National AI Policy, Sept 2025, pp.3, 7](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf)
- The AI Committee was established by Cabinet Decision No. (10) of 2021. A **National Center for AI** under MCIT was approved by a draft Cabinet decision in September 2025, to coordinate national AI strategy and regulatory and infrastructure projects. — [Eversheds Sutherland, Nov 2025](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/united-states/insights/mapping-qatars-regulatory-landscape-for-artificial-intelligence-november-2025)
- **Cabinet Decision No. 34 of 2026** (approved by the Emir; reported 20 Oct 2026) reorganises MCIT and creates the **National Center for Artificial Intelligence (NCAI)**. It reports directly to the MCIT Minister and has departments for strategy, platform development and strategic partnerships. Its mandate: prepare the national AI strategy and follow its implementation; "develop policies and regulatory frameworks for responsible and safe AI use"; provide studies to government entities; build AI applications; and help organisations adopt AI. — [Middle East AI News, Oct 2026](https://www.middleeastainews.com/p/qatar-national-center-for-ai) *(secondary; official gazette text not seen)*
- The 2019 strategy began as a blueprint by the **Qatar Computing Research Institute (QCRI, HBKU)**, adopted as the national strategy. MCIT describes six pillars: education, data access, employment, business, research and ethics. — [HBKU](https://hbku.edu.qa/en/node/13389); [MCIT AI Committee page](https://mcit.gov.qa/en/node/20955) *(both from search extracts; pages not opened)*
- In February 2026 MCIT and the QRDI Council launched an "Artificial Intelligence for Qatar" research programme aligned with the National AI Strategy (research funding, not regulation). — [The Peninsula, 15 Feb 2026](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/15/02/2026/qatar-unveils-national-ai-programme-to-accelerate-research-and-economic-growth) *(not opened)*

The list was not seen, so it is unclear whether the AI Policy was among them. — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/technology/1725700/ministry-of-communications-and-information-technology-launches-public-consultation-on-digital-economy-policies) *(not opened)*

**NCSA Guidelines for Secure Adoption and Usage of AI (Feb 2024)**
- Launched 19 February 2024 by NCSA as a 36-page Arabic/English publication. It is a people, process and technology framework aligned to the AI lifecycle, with strategic questions for adoption (need, selection, what to secure, value, verification), security best practices for new or existing systems, and digital-trust topics (security, privacy, ethics, governance, responsible use). GenAI is explicitly covered. Organisations may choose the practices and controls suited to their environment, which makes them voluntary. — [The Peninsula, 20 Feb 2024](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/20/02/2024/ncsa-launches-guidelines-for-secure-adoption-and-use-of-ai); [Sultan Al-Abdulla & Partners](https://qatarlaw.com/news/the-national-cyber-security-agency-launches-guidelines-for-safe-artificial-intelligence-use)
- A Qatar University law professor's IDPD 2026 deck (hosted on NCSA's site) summarises the NCSA Guidelines' risk taxonomy:
  - (1) unauthorised disclosure: breach, data aggregation/AI inference, "data retention by AI models", and attacks such as "Membership Inference, jailbreaking, prompt leaking, token smuggling";
  - (2) unauthorised modification: model poisoning, model extraction, "data supply chain integrity";
  - (3) non-availability;
  - (4) privacy violations: surveillance/profiling, regulatory non-compliance, data sharing;
  - (5) data ethics and bias.

  GenAI guidance covers data privacy and security, security awareness, security hygiene, and "Prevent sensitive data leakage with GenAI use policy". "Secure and Trusted AI" controls are grouped as People (ethical use, governance, oversight), Process (transparency, documentation, monitoring, auditability, reporting; data observability and reliability) and Technology (resilient AI, access/supply-chain security, review of data aggregation, "Implement federated learning model", "Differential privacy for aggregated data"). — [Dr. Rafael Dean Brown (Qatar University College of Law), "Implementing Responsible and Compliant AI Systems", IDPD 2026, NCSA-hosted, slides 9–12, 22](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10209/1.0~926129d6-931a-48dd-b185-7e15bb07e2fa?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Implementing+Responsible+and+Compliant+AI+Systems+-+IDPD+2026+%2810%29.pdf)
- The guidelines apply as best practice "across all public and private organisations". — [DCO Qatar Country Report, p.32](https://dco.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Qatar-Report.pdf)
- Binding status is disputed. Most sources treat the guidelines as non-binding. One secondary source says organisations are assessed against them. — [regulations.ai entry](https://regulations.ai/regulations/RAI-QA-NA-GSAUAXX-2024); [theleveragedyears (secondary)](https://www.theleveragedyears.com/ai-regulation-news/qatar-ncsa-secure-adoption-usage-ai-guidelines)

**MCIT ethics instruments**
- "Principles and Guidelines for Ethical Development and Deployment of Artificial Intelligence (MCIT, 2024)". The QU deck quotes:
  - Principle 1, Do no harm: test for "bias, hallucinations, data poisoning, and adversarial attacks".
  - Principle 2, Robust and secure: "encrypted training data and anonymized personal data".
  - Principle 3, Reduce bias: "representative data; rectify inaccurate and biased datasets".
  - Principle 5, Safeguard privacy: protect special-nature data; data-access protocols with DPOs; consent or opt-out options; let users flag privacy issues.
  - Principle 6, Promote transparency: about the system and data collection, input and use.
  - Principle 8, **Assign ultimate accountability to humans**: "human control over AI system and data protection".

**Draft National AI Policy (MCIT, Sept 2025; consultation, not in force)**

**AI and PDPPL interplay (as presented at NCSA's IDPD 2026)**
- PDPPL Art. 1 defines personal data as data of an individual "whose identity is defined or can be reasonably defined whether through such Personal Data or through the combination of such data with any other data". "Processing" includes organisation, preparation, modification, usage, disclosure and transfer, so AI inference creating special-nature attributes (Art. 16, e.g. health) requires prior permission. Recommendations: "Treat inferred data as personal data" and "Extend DPIAs to inference risk". — [QU/NCSA-hosted IDPD 2026 deck, slides 15–25](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10209/1.0~926129d6-931a-48dd-b185-7e15bb07e2fa?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Implementing+Responsible+and+Compliant+AI+Systems+-+IDPD+2026+%2810%29.pdf)

### Inferences
- An external AI diagnostic service is an LLM/GenAI or ML vendor relationship. The NCSA Guidelines' named risks map straight onto the gateway's purpose: "data retention by AI models", prompt leaking, membership inference and supply-chain integrity. The gateway is a technical control for the GenAI section's "prevent sensitive data leakage" practice and for MCIT Principle 2's "anonymized personal data".
- The PDPPL "reasonably defined… combination with any other data" test, together with the inference argument, means de-identified outputs returned from the AI (e.g. a diagnosis attached to a pseudonym) become identifiable health data again once re-linked inside the hospital. The gateway's audit trail should cover the return path too.
- Human oversight is not legally mandated for hospitals today. MCIT Principle 8, draft NAIP s.5.5.5 and the QCB human-oversight taxonomy (as an analogy) all point toward a clinician-in-the-loop design.

## 7. Automated decision-making, algorithmic transparency and AI procurement for government/semi-government entities

### Takeaway
No binding Qatari rule on automated decision-making (ADM) or algorithmic transparency was found for onshore public bodies. The PDPPL has no GDPR Art. 22 equivalent identified by sources. The most specific provisions are in the **draft** National AI Policy (Sept 2025):
- ex-ante risk/opacity classification of public-sector AI;
- a ban on high-risk or opaque systems making consequential decisions without human oversight;
- mandatory AI-interaction notices for government digital services;
- mandatory redress mechanisms;
- strengthened AI procurement and RFP capacity.

Separately, the CRA framework recommends that government cloud procurement follow Cloud-First.

### Cited Findings
- Draft NAIP s.5.5.5 "AI and Decision-Making Processes": "the government shall assess AI systems in advance (ex-ante) and classify them based on their level of risk, opacity, and explainability. This classification shall guide their appropriate use across public institutions, ensuring that high-risk or opaque systems are not allowed to make consequential decisions without human oversight." — [MCIT draft NAIP](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf)
- s.5.5.6: "AI governance instances shall devise and deploy mandatory redress mechanisms to ensure individuals or entities negatively impacted by algorithm decisions have a way to appeal them." — [MCIT draft NAIP](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf)
- s.5.5.3: "Each public institution shall have a similar mechanism [AI governance instance] at the AI implementation level." s.5.5.1–5.5.2 propose a supra-ministerial AI Policy Unit and "risk classification frameworks and impact evaluations". — [MCIT draft NAIP](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf)
- s.5.4.3: strengthen "procurement capabilities and equipping public entities with adequate competencies needed to design and manage advanced AI tenders and requests for proposals (RFPs)". s.5.4.5: regular AI impact assessment of public-sector implementation (public value). s.6.2: public-sector entities must "Ensure NAIP is appropriately implemented within their institutions". — [MCIT draft NAIP](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf)
- s.5.3.6: AI-interaction notice "shall be mandatory for government institutions and entities that provide digital services and information". — [MCIT draft NAIP](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf)
- The PDPPL section of the Eversheds review does not address ADM. ADM-specific DPIA duties appear only in the **QFC Data Protection Regulations (2021)**, a separate financial free-zone regime. — [Eversheds Sutherland, Nov 2025](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/united-states/insights/mapping-qatars-regulatory-landscape-for-artificial-intelligence-november-2025)

The NCSA/PwC deck mentions an updated Open Data Policy v2.0.0. — [MCIT draft NAIP](https://prod16-assets.sprinklr.com/prod16-cdata/DAM/160136/9d77e677-a7f4-4659-a890-8e723c53e6dc-1081146622/MCIT_National_AI_Policy_Consul.pdf); [NCSA-hosted PwC deck](https://ncsa.gov.qa/shared/document_library/94787172324409/33000/10207/1.0~fc4efdb1-0710-4db9-a751-c48e068c9509?mimetype=application%2Fpdf&filename=Presentation+-+Data+Privacy+in+Medical+Research+-+IDPD+2026+%282%29.pdf)

### Inferences
- If the NAIP is adopted largely as drafted, a public hospital's AI diagnostic service would likely face ex-ante risk classification (clinical diagnosis would almost certainly be high-risk), a human-oversight requirement for consequential decisions, patient-facing AI notices and an appeal/redress route. A gateway that logs each AI call and records the clinician's sign-off would make those obligations easier to evidence.
- Public hospitals buying a foreign AI service would also go through government procurement and Cloud-First endorsement channels (endorsed CSPs), and NIA third-party scoping.

### Gaps
- I found no Ministry of Finance / government procurement rule specific to AI, and no published list of "endorsed" cloud providers.
- I could not determine whether HMC, PHCC or Sidra count as "government entities" for Cloud-First and NDCP purposes in a specific legal instrument. They are generally described as public or semi-government, but no source states it.
- The NAIP's post-consultation status as of November 2026 is unknown.
